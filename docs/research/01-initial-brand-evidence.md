# Initial Minimalist brand evidence

Status: **Evidence draft for human review — not a brand rulebook**

Date collected: 2026-09-11

Collection target after this initial pass: all verifiable unique current
creatives that can reasonably be discovered. Twenty static image ads and twenty
text ads, distributed across product types, are minimum coverage checks rather
than collection limits.

## Why this document exists

The scorer must evaluate unseen Minimalist creatives against a documented
standard. This document records the evidence before converting observations
into rules. It deliberately separates:

- what Minimalist explicitly says;
- what was directly observed in current product copy;
- what a third party says appears in current ads;
- contradictions and unanswered questions; and
- hypotheses that still require human review.

## 1. Explicitly stated brand principles

### Transparency and opposition to fearmongering

Minimalist's `Our values` page says the beauty industry suffers from inaccurate
advice and incorrect claims that result in “fear mongering” and misconceptions.
It frames the brand's response as products that are straightforward and honest,
without unnecessary marketing fluff.

Source:
[Minimalist — Our values](https://beminimalist.co/pages/our-values)

What this supports:

- Avoiding fearmongering is an explicit brand intention.
- Straightforwardness, honesty, and transparency are explicit intentions.

What this does **not** support:

- It does not prove that every published Minimalist execution follows these
  intentions.
- It does not prove that Minimalist's own paid ads contain fearmongering.
- It does not yet define an operational test for fearmongering.

### Simple, honest, and science-backed communication

The current `About` page describes the aspiration as making skincare simple,
honest, and backed by science, eliminating guesswork, and helping people make
informed choices. It also defines transparency as being clear about both
ingredients and communication.

Source:
[Minimalist — About](https://beminimalist.co/pages/about)

Candidate interpretation, not yet a rule:

- Copy should help the reader understand what a product contains, what it is
  intended to do, and what to expect.
- Scientific language should clarify a decision rather than merely decorate a
  claim.

## 2. Current product-copy examples

### Example A — Niacinamide 10% Face Serum

Observed structure:

- Product name leads with an ingredient and concentration.
- The short benefit line names specific concerns: sebum, pores, and uneven
  tone.
- Supporting copy links Niacinamide and Matmarine to proposed functions.
- Additional details include pH, usage, ingredient supplier, and a two-week
  efficacy statement.

Source:
[Minimalist — Niacinamide 10% Face Serum](https://beminimalist.co/products/niacinamide-10-with-matmarine)

Notable language:

- Precise: `Niacinamide 10% Face Serum`, `pH: 5.5–6.5`.
- Mechanistic: references sebum regulation, protein synthesis, and melanin
  concentration.
- Strong or potentially risky: `clinically proven ... in 2 weeks`, `perfect
  biotechnological ingredient`, `reduces inflammation`.
- A customer testimonial says acne marks are “almost gone.” A testimonial is
  not equivalent to an approved brand claim and must be treated separately.

### Example B — Retinol 0.3% Face Serum

Observed structure:

- Product name leads with an ingredient and concentration.
- The page explains formulation stability and ingredient roles.
- Usage guidance includes frequency, sunscreen use, and an age restriction.
- Some benefits are qualified with phrases such as `visibly` and `in some
  studies`.

Source:
[Minimalist — Retinol 0.3% Face Serum](https://beminimalist.co/products/retinol-0-3-q10)

Notable language:

- Precise: `0.3% pure Retinol`, `1% pure Bakuchiol Oil`.
- Qualified: `in some studies has shown` and `visibly reduces`.
- Strong or potentially risky: `powerful anti-aging`, `repairs skin`, and
  `most effective substance`.

These examples show that the current site mixes precise scientific language
with conventional promotional language. We should not infer a “never use
powerful language” rule from the brand manifesto alone.

## 3. Where fear- or shame-related language was found

No directly inspected paid ad has yet been classified as fearmongering.

The concrete examples found are from an older Minimalist editorial article:

- Its URL/title includes `tightening ugly pores`.
- It tells readers they “can't seem to tighten” pores because they are not
  cleaning and exfoliating regularly.
- It describes acids as a “holy grail,” says some ingredients will not
  disappoint, and makes strong treatment-like statements about acne and
  blackheads.

Source:
[Minimalist — How to minimize open pores on your face](https://beminimalist.co/blogs/skin-care/10-minutes-of-skincare-routine-for-tightening-ugly-pores)

Why this is only a review candidate:

- `Ugly pores` could shame a normal skin characteristic and appears inconsistent
  with the stated anti-fearmongering position.
- The article is old editorial content, not evidence of current paid-ad tone.
- Calling this “fearmongering” requires an operational definition we have not
  agreed upon. `Anxiety- or shame-coded` is the more accurate provisional label.

Another older editorial article discusses the industry's ideas about harmful
or toxic ingredients while also challenging simplistic `natural equals safe`
claims. Its overall stance is nuanced, but individual passages may still use
alarm-oriented framing.

Source:
[Minimalist — Clean Beauty Means Safe and Sustainable Beauty](https://beminimalist.co/blogs/skin-care/clean-beauty)

## 4. Paid-ad evidence collected so far

Update on 2026-09-11: the exact filtered official Meta Ad Library page was
captured. Eighty accessible Library records yielded 94 creative images, 75
exact file hashes, and 51 automated candidate concept groups. Twenty static
executions across product categories were directly inspected. See
`current-ads/static/META_REFERENCE_REVIEW.md` and
`03-candidate-scoring-standard.md`.

The Motion index claims to track recent Meta ads for Minimalist and describes a
mix of manufacturing footage, product demonstrations, testimonials/creator
content, before-and-after content, and offer-first static banners.

Source:
[Motion — Minimalist paid social creative strategy](https://motionapp.com/library/minimalistinc)

Static concepts visible in the third-party descriptions:

1. An offer banner showing three products and a free-SPF promotion, reportedly
   headlined `Grab Your Freebie!`.
2. A sensitive-skin product grouping on a white background.
3. A Hydrating Factors 7.3% shampoo feature-benefit creative using terms such
   as `non-stripping`, `gentle`, and `sulfate-free`.

These descriptions challenge an overly narrow assumption that every Minimalist
ad is education-first or purely clinical. However, because the underlying
static images have not yet been directly reviewed, they are leads rather than
rule-setting evidence.

## 5. Policy source found so far

The ASCI Code says advertising representations and claims should be truthful
and honest and should not mislead consumers. It also states that the Code
complements rather than replaces legal controls.

Source:
[Advertising Standards Council of India — The ASCI Code](https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code/)

The ASCI guidelines separately address disclaimers and skin-lightening ads. In
particular, a disclaimer should not repair an otherwise misleading headline,
and skin-lightening advertising should not associate darker skin with
inferiority, unhappiness, or disadvantage.

Source:
[Advertising Standards Council of India — Code Guidelines](https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code-guidelines/)

No legal rule has yet been encoded. A fuller primary-source legal review is
still required.

## 6. Hypotheses for later review

These are **not approved rules**:

1. Ingredient and concentration are often prominent parts of product identity.
2. Stronger brand-fit copy connects an ingredient or formulation fact to a
   specific concern or expected benefit.
3. Specificity, usage guidance, and qualification are more characteristic than
   generic transformation promises.
4. The brand's format range is broader than a purely clinical aesthetic; offers
   and creator-style executions may be valid.
5. Shame, fear, and misinformation are inconsistent with stated brand intent,
   but we still need precise definitions and current-ad examples.

## 7. Evidence required before proposing rules

- Define the sampling categories before collecting more examples.
- Directly inspect 15–20 unique current ads per agreed category; variants of one
  creative concept do not count as separate evidence.
- Sample more product categories, including sunscreen, pigmentation, haircare,
  moisturizers, and products that do not feature an active concentration.
- Separate packaging language, product-page language, paid-ad language, and
  customer testimonials.
- Record publication/observation dates and avoid treating old editorial copy as
  current guidance.
- Create candidate rules only after the evidence packet is reviewed with the
  user.
